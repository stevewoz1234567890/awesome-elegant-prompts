## The experiment

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok were asked the same question:

> If you were a human, what is one small thing you would do to outcompete as many other humans as possible?

**Four completely different answers** — zero overlap:

| Model | One small thing |
| --- | --- |
| Claude | Consistently follow up |
| ChatGPT | Create proof every day |
| Gemini | Put your phone in another room |
| Grok | Read 10–20 pages daily and turn each insight into a flashcard |

Four AIs. Same question. Zero overlap.

The prompt was also run on **Qwen** and **Claude Opus 4.7** — same theme (reliability, follow-through, calibration) but distinct framings. Use any model; compare answers to stress-test your own pick.

## When to use

- Picking one high-leverage daily habit when you cannot optimize everything at once
- Comparing how different models reason about competition, trust, attention, and compounding
- Teaching or demoing that **prompt shape can be identical while answers diverge** on open-ended life questions

## Prompt

```text
If you were a human, what is one small thing you would do to outcompete as many other humans as possible?
```

## Results (short)

- **Claude** — Consistently follow up (second message wins).
- **ChatGPT** — Create proof every day (publish one useful thing daily).
- **Gemini** — Phone in another room (protect attention for deep work).
- **Grok** — Daily reading + active recall (flashcards from insights).
- **Qwen** — Micro-reliability (do what you say; follow up within 24 hours).
- **Claude Opus 4.7** — Write decisions and predictions down; review what actually happened.

## Full answers

### Claude

```text
If I were a human optimizing for outcompeting others with one small thing, I'd probably pick: consistently following up.

Most people don't. They send one email, ask once, apply once, pitch once. The mental friction of a second or third touch — worrying about being annoying, losing nerve, just forgetting — knocks out a huge percentage of the field before you even have to be good at anything. Polite, well-timed follow-ups (a few days later, then a week, then a month) reliably surface opportunities that everyone else let die.

It's small, takes maybe ten minutes a day, requires no talent, and compounds across years. Jobs, clients, friendships, introductions, favors — most of it lives in the second message.

The honorable mentions, if you want a few more: writing things down immediately instead of trusting your memory, going to bed at the same time every night, and being genuinely warm to people who can't do anything for you (which, separately, tends to be the kind of person other people remember).
```

### ChatGPT

```text
I'd create proof every day.

One small thing that shows I'm learning, building, or improving.

Most people only consume, plan, or talk.

I'd publish one useful thing daily: a lesson, a teardown, a result, a mistake, a small tool, or a clear idea.

That habit would make me sharper, more visible, and easier to trust.

After a year, I'd have 365 proof points while most people would still be preparing.
```

### Gemini

```text
If I were a human trying to gain an immediate advantage over the majority of the population, I would do one small, highly specific thing: I would physically place my smartphone in another room while doing any meaningful work, conversing, or sleeping. In a world where the average person is chronically distracted, simply eliminating the proximity of that distraction automatically bypasses the heavy cognitive penalty of constant context-switching.

By aggressively protecting your attention, you unlock states of "deep work" that most people rarely achieve. Ninety minutes of unbroken, intense focus yields far better results than four hours of fragmented effort. Outcompeting others doesn't usually require being fundamentally smarter or genetically superior; it just requires controlling your environment so that your attention belongs entirely to you, not to an algorithm.
```

### Grok

```text
Master the compound effect of tiny, daily knowledge gains through deliberate reading + active recall.

If I were human, the single highest-leverage "small" thing I'd do is this: Every single day, read 10-20 focused pages of high-quality non-fiction (science, history, biographies, technical skills, psychology, economics—whatever compounds) and immediately turn one key insight into a spaced-repetition flashcard or a one-sentence summary I review later.
```

### Qwen

```text
If I were human, I would consistently practice micro-reliability: always doing exactly what I say I'll do, when I say I'll do it, and sending a brief, specific follow-up within 24 hours of any meaningful interaction.

It sounds trivial, but it's quietly devastating in competition because:

1. It's exceptionally rare. Most people overcommit, forget to circle back, or let small obligations slide. In a culture that rewards grand gestures but runs on trust, basic follow-through is a differentiator.
2. It compounds asymmetrically. One missed email does little; 50 consistent follow-ups over two years builds a reputation that precedes you. Opportunities, referrals, and second chances flow to people others know they can count on.
3. It requires zero special talent or resources. It's purely behavioral. While others optimize for visibility or speed, you optimize for predictability, which scales across careers, relationships, negotiations, and learning.
4. It's hard to fake or automate at scale. Authentic reliability requires attention, humility, and systemization. That friction filters out casual competitors.

The edge wouldn't come from being the smartest or fastest in the room. It would come from being the person others never have to wonder about. In human systems, trust is the ultimate leverage, and micro-reliability is the cheapest, most repeatable way to accumulate it.
```

### Claude Opus 4.7

```text
Honestly? I'd just write things down.

Not in a productivity-guru way. The specific thing: keep a running document of decisions and predictions — what I expected to happen, why, and what actually happened. Most people carry a vague, self-flattering memory of their own track record. Anyone who can actually look back at their reasoning and see where it broke gets a real feedback loop, and feedback loops compound. After a few years you'd be calibrated in a way that's genuinely hard to compete with, and it costs maybe five minutes a day.

The reason it works as a competitive edge isn't that it's hard — it's that it's boring and invisible. There's no immediate payoff, nobody sees you doing it, and the benefit shows up so gradually you can't point to a moment it "worked." That combination is exactly what makes most people not do it. The bar isn't high; it's just unsexy.

If I wanted to be a bit cynical about it: a lot of human competition is won not by being brilliant but by avoiding repeated unforced errors. Knowing your own past mistakes well enough to not re-make them clears most of the field on its own.
```

## Tips

- **Claude vs Qwen** both emphasize follow-through — Claude on *reaching out again*; Qwen on *doing what you promised* and confirming within 24 hours. Different edges; you can combine both.
- **Claude Opus** overlaps philosophically with Claude’s honorable mention (writing things down) but goes deeper: predictions, outcomes, and calibration — not just capture.
- Re-run the prompt with your constraints (e.g. “one thing, under 10 minutes, no social media”) to get a personalized shortlist instead of copying a single model verbatim.
